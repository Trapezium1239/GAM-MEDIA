[128] This chapter reviews the existing literature on adolescent mental health, focusing on depression, mental health literacy, and the role of religious institutions in supporting mental health. The review will explore the prevalence of mental health disorders among adolescents, the impact of depression, and the effectiveness of mental health interventions. Additionally, it will assess the potential of religious organisations in addressing mental health needs through training programmes for adolescent educators.
[129] This study was based on the study of (Habeb, Ciobanu, Al-Ani, and Mottershead, 2025) in understanding the impact of stigma in mental health where they defined stigma as a way of thinking by which the reaction of some people toward others could ruin their confidence and identity. They pointed out that people living with mental illnesses may be perceived by those who stigmatize as displaying unacceptable social or mental attribute during social interactions. Stigma is common in psychiatric care and it is distinguished by negative stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination that result from misconceptions about mental illness. From their study, they revealed that living with mental illness carries a stigma that patients have described to be worse than the illness itself. They reported that stronger forms of stigma results in social anxiety where patients are concerned about how others perceive them, their behavior, appearance or speech. According to the authors, stigma remarkably detains access to timely and appropriate mental healthcare as patients tend to withdraw from social situations in order to avoid negative reactions of being laughed at, criticized or rejected. As a result of this, people suffer more, their cases also are left untreated and this leads to a bad outcome such as high morbidity, low quality of life, and suicide in the long run. 
[130] Depression as a mental illness was believed by Rey and Bella-Awusah (2015) to pose a substantial burden to the individual suffering from it and to the society at large. They noted that not only the individuals with depression suffer but because these individuals would withdraw socially, become emotionally distant, they become irritable, prefer to stay on their own, interpersonal relationships are likely to suffer. They also reported that when a person is depressed, it is likely to progress into a chronic, recurring disease if not treated. In young people, early identification and management are very important in the intervention for depression. These promote reduced severity, improves outcome, prevents complications, reduces physical health complications and over all, improves quality of life.  Beames, Kikas and Werner-Seidler (2021) stated that “prevention and timely early intervention efforts are necessary to lessen the burden of depression in young people.” The understanding of the members of the society on depression would increase chances of early identification and intervention in young people who may present with this disorder. When awareness is increased in the society, it improves understanding and acceptance of the people with this disorder. It further leads to a cooperative effort in management, stigma reduction, enhances open conversation around the disorder, encourage help seeking behaviors and treatment adherence, and reduces judgment. This in turn improves the overall outcome of the young people with the disorder.
[132] Prevalence of Mental Health Disorders among Adolescents
[133] Adolescence is a critical period for the onset of mental health disorders, with depression being the most common conditions (Schulte-Körne, 2016). Mental health disorders are common in this period because of factors such as the biological, psychological and social factors. The biological factors involve significant brain development, hormonal changes related to puberty, which makes the adolescent more vulnerable to mental health issues like depression.
[134] The psychological factors involve self-esteem issues, emotional regulation problems, and identity issues. The adolescent is usually a period where self-identity is built and the way adolescents perceive themselves at this period is very important to their mental health. Environmental factors also involve the interaction of the adolescent with their immediate environments. Events happening around them at this period can either improve or affect their mental health. Factors such as abuse; physical and emotional, poverty, the loss of loved ones, school and relationship stress, cyberbullying, family dynamics such as parental divorce or separation can all affect the adolescents’ mental health and make them susceptible to depression. 
[135] Research shows that  about 10% to 20% of adolescents worldwide experience mental health issues, with varying degrees of severity (Nebhinani and Jain, 2019). The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that depression account for a significant portion of the global burden of disease among adolescents, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (WHO, 2021). In low and middle income countries, the rate of depression is high among adolescents because of factors such as high level of poverty, which limits the adolescents’ access to education and health care servicesAdolescent’s exposure to violence such as abuse and community violence, cultural expectations, religious beliefs and practices all increase the risk of depression in the adolescents Additionally, lack of trained mental health professionals in this region, inadequate health care infrastructure and stigma significantly contribute to the increased statistics of depression among adolescents in the  countries.
[136] Depression in adolescents due to the level of impairment it causes is raising a lot of concerns among public health problemsSome studies have reported that depression carries a prevalence of 5-15%, affecting 1 in 5 people before adulthood. It has also been shown in previous studies that 3% to 9% of adolescents meet the criteria for depression at a point in their lives while about 20% of teenagers report a lifetime prevalence of depression by the end of adolescence (Lu, Lin and Su, 2024). Worldwide, epidemiological surveys reported about 2.6% children and adolescents to be suffering from depression (Polanczyk, Salum, Sugaya, Caye and Rohde, 2015). In some western countries, the prevalence of depression has been recorded to be as high as 5.5% to 11.3% for a period of twelve months (Tang, Ren and Tang, 2019).  In a cross-sectional study by  Berhane, Canavan, Darling, Sudfeld, Vuai, Adanu, Barnighausen, Dessie, Bukenya, Guwatudde, Killewo, Sando, Sie, Oduola and Fawzi (2020) in nine communities in BurkinaFaso, Ethiopia, Eswatini, Ghana, Nigeria, Tanzania and Uganda it was reported that the prevalence of low mood was generally higher among older adolescents (15 to 19 years) than younger adolescents (10 to 14 years).
[137] , the prevalence of depression among young people is high both in the developed and in the developing countries (Shorey, Ng, and Wong, 2022). At pre-pubertal period, the rate of depression among males and females are similar but during the adolescence period, it becomes twice as common in females than males (Rey, Bella-awusah and Liu, 2015). Although the exact reason for this is not known but studies have suggested that it may be due to females experiencing more psychosocial stress and hormonal changes than males (Barker, Beresford, and Fraser 2023). For females, menstruation starts at this period and fluctuation of estrogen and progesterone during menstrual cycle can lead to mood swings, irritability and anxiety. Teenage pregnancy at this period, societal expectations form females also poses stress on females and increases the risk of mental disorders. 
[138] . A study byThapar, Eyre, Patel and Brent, 2022) reported the relationship between depression and factors such as physical abuse, sexual abuse, bereavement, parental loss or separation, divorce, poverty and violence within the community.
[139] Furthermore, studies have also shown that adolescents with chronic medical and physical diseases such as diabetics, cancer are susceptible to depression (Barker, Beresford, and Fraser 2023). Chronic medical illnesses in adolescents can lead to chronic pains which can lead to intense sadness, sleep disturbance which causes disruption in mood regulations, medication side effectome medications can increase depression rate, and limited interaction with the environment; chronic conditions can limit movements and activities, resulting in feelings of abandonment, isolation, hopelessness and intense sadness.
[140] In Nigeria, adolescents face unique challenges due to socio-economic pressures, political instability, and cultural barriers, all of which contribute to increased rates of depression (Aluh, Anyachebelu, Anosike and Anizoba, 2018). However, due to limited access to mental health services and the stigma surrounding mental health disorders in Low and Middle Income countries of which Nigeria is one, many cases go undiagnosed and untreated (Alukagberie, Elmusharaf, Ibrahim and Poix, 2023). 
[142] In treatment and management of depression, two approaches are employed to address the symptoms and improve the quality of life of the patient; the pharmacology approach (the use of anti-depressants), and also Psychological Therapy, (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) (Pannu, and Goyal, 2025). A study by (Miller, and Campo, 2021) reported the effectiveness of the use of antidepressants for treating depression in adolescents. Another study reported the use of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) as being very effective in the treatment of depression (Oud, De Winter, Vermeulen-Smit, Bodden, Nauta, Stone, Van Den Heuvel, Al Taher, De Graaf, Kendall, and Engels, 2019). Depression in adolescents is usually characterized by unhealthy feelings of hopelessness, guilt, life being meaningless among othersCBT focuses on equipping the adolescents with healthy coping skills, addressing maladaptive behaviours, helping them identify and understand their own emotions and relate effectively with people (Grossberg and Rice, 2023).
[144] Generally, the early treatment and intervention for depression is very important because of the impact it has on adolescents. For instance, if an adolescent with depression is left untreated, it may develop into personality disorder in the long run, interfering with the developing personality and also further increase the risk of suicide (Oud, De Winter, Vermeulen-Smit, Bodden, Nauta, Stone, Van Den Heuvel, Al Taher, De Graaf, Kendall, and Engels, 2019).
[146]  2.3 Impact of Depression on Adolescents 
[147] Depression profoundly affects adolescents’ physical, social, and cognitive development. It raises lots of concerns, which present a huge burden not only on the individual suffering from it, but also on their immediate family, and the society at large. When a young person is depressed, relationship within the family is strained, family members suffer and not just the patient (Rey, Bella-Awusah and Liu, 2015).  Depressed adolescents may become irritable, presenting with anger outbursts, crying uncontrollably and mood swings, they may also tend to withdraw from their immediate families, become unable to communicate effectively, they may act and behave irrationally, making openness and interaction difficult. Parents’ and family’s misinterpretation of the disorder may lead to judgment, stigma and violence which may worsen the presentation of the disorder.  
[148] Of all mental illnesses, depression is one of the most disabling and it has a notable impact on society (Gutiérrez-Rojas, Porras-Segovia, Dunne, Andrade-González, and Cervilla, 2020), it is a leading cause of disability worldwide, it is common and could be fatal. It is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality globally (Grossberg and Rice, 2023). Stigma surrounding depression increases fear of judgment and misinterpretation of the illness and tempers help seeking behavior.  Limited access to health care, poverty and inequalities, lack of awareness, all delay early intervention and contributes immensely to the impact of the disorder on adolescents. 
[149]  Mental disorders are reported to contribute to 13 percent of the global burden of disease and by 2030, depression is expected to be the greatest contributor (Arias, Saxena and Verguet, 2022). Depression affects the psychosocial and school-related development of adolescents in major ways; it has a significant impact on an individual’s ability to carry out life activities, leading to a reduction in productivity and work performance (Gunnarsson, Hedberg, Håkansson, Hedin, and Wagman, P., 2023).
[150] Depression is among the most common mental health disorders which significantly impair a person’s functionality, starting in early or mid-adolescence. It can have profound effects on adolescents' emotional, social, and academic development (Rice, Eyre, Riglin and Potter, 2017). In terms of academic performance, depression reduces concentration, decreases motivation and interest, reduces attention span, affects problem solving and decision makings, impairs learning and retention of informationThis leads to the adolescents struggling to keep their grades up and meet academic expectations, loss of interest in school, which increases the risk of having to repeat a year and dropping out of school. It may also lead to stigma both in school and in the community, impair patient’s ability to work and organize work, impair their memory function and this may lead to doubts about one’s own perceived ability (Schulte-körne, 2016). Adolescents with this condition often experience impaired concentration, fatigue, and decreased motivation, which can hinder their academic performance and limit their potential (Schulte-Körne, 2016). Additionally, depression can lead to social withdrawal, strained relationships with peers and family, and increased feelings of isolation (Velasco, Cruz, Billings, Jimenez and Rowe, 2020).
[151] Depression does not only significantly affects the adolescent’s socialization, relationship among families and peers and school performance, it may also have potential long-term consequences on patientsDepression may increase the risk of personality disorder, increase low self-esteem, difficulty forming and sustaining relationships, delayed development of social and problem solving skills, reduced productivity, lower academic achievement, limited career achievement and difficulty choosing a career path. Depression may also lead to the development of chronic illnesses such as cardiovascular diseases, sleep disturbances, nutritional deficiencies due to loss of appetite, the immune system may also become weak, making the individual’s body prone to physical illnesses like Malaria. Halonen, Hakko, Riala and Riipinen (2022) found evidence that depressed adolescents may be at a high risk of increased hospitalization, recurrent depressive episodes, alcohol and substance abuse problems, self-harm, psychosocial impairment, and antisocial behaviors, but of all the effects, the most devastating is suicide.  
[152] Depression, in particular, is closely linked to increased risk of suicidal ideation and attempts among adolescents. The emotional stress that comes with depression may flood the adolescents’ coping skills, result in social isolation, loss of pleasure in things that were formerly pleasurable, increase negative intrusive thoughts, and results in the depressed individual acting impulsively and increase hopelessness which fuels suicidal thoughts. Suicide remains one of the leading causes of death in this age group, particularly in developing countries where access to mental health care is limited ( In developing countries, lack of trained mental health professionals, reduced access to health care, limited availability to mental health support contributes to high suicide rates.
[153] In a study by Carballo, Llorente, Kehrmann, Flamarique, Zuddas, Ouakil, Hoekstra, Coghill, Schulze, Dittmann, Buitelaar, Castro-Fornieles, Lievesley, Santosh, Arango (2019), approximately, one million people complete suicide every year. It is the third leading cause of death in young people (ages 10-29). Depression, although not present in all suicide cases in this population, is still a strong risk factor and the mortality risk for suicide in depressed patients is more than twenty times greater than in the general population. (Yang and Lee, 2020) for instance, following up 260 depressed patients reported a significantly higher suicidal risk and suicidal attempts among 189 patients who were untreated. Similarly, Lourenço, Rosana, Flavia and Everton (2015) in a study conducted among 1,379 students reported the prevalence of the risk of suicide to be 29.7%  of which those with depressive symptoms were at the greatest risk. Depression does not always lead to suicide but it is a strong risk factor for completed suicide. 
[154] Depression may occur independently or may co-morbid with other disorders like anxiety, substance misuse among others (Cosci and Fava, 2021).  Alauddin, Rana, Ibne, Maksud, Kabir, and Mollah, (2024), in a cross-sectional 150 adolescents age 12 to 19 years over one year. The study revealed high prevalence and comorbidity of anxiety and depression (55.33% and 44.67% respectively), with substance use disorder being the most common comorbidity with anxiety and depression. Depression may also comorbid with attention deficit, which makes the depressed individual unable to retain information, bipolar disorder, eating disorders like bulimia nervosa, which may result in diabetes, a medical comorbidity (Wal, Kumar, Bhardwaj, Sharma, Tripathi, Gupta, Wal, and Sharma, 2025). Depression also may comorbid with Obsessive Compulsive disorders which increases recurring intrusive thoughts and compulsionThe long-term effects of untreated depression include chronic mental health problems, substance misuse, and poor life outcomes in adulthood (Nebhinani and Jain, 2019). When depression is left untreated in adolescents, it predisposes them to lower academic performance, poor career choices, underemployment, poor job satisfaction, separation and divorce, poverty, later in adulthood. 
[155] In addition, when depression is left untreated, it leads to the adolescent developing personality disorders later in life as the adolescent period is the time where personality traits are being formed (Oud, De Winter, Vermeulen-Smit, Bodden, Nauta, Stone, Van Den Heuvel, Al Taher, De Graaf, Kendall, and Engels, 2019).
[156] In the society, the impact of depression can be attributed to the inability of patients to contribute meaningfully and also direct cost of treatment. For instance, in India, mental disorders cost nearly a third of the projected 47 trillion US dollars incurred by all non-communicable disease (Menon, Yadav, and John, 2022). Also, depression in adolescent is associated with higher risk of problems with the law, more negative life events and teen pregnancies (Bodden, Stikkelbroek and Dirksen, 2018).
[158] 2.4 Causes and Risk Factors of Depression
[159] Depression has its peak incidence in mid-to-late adolescence and this is because the challenges young people experience increase drastically during this period, especially for girlsChallenges such as family conflicts, social isolation, cyberbullying, academic stress, relationship changes and heartbreaks, navigating puberty and handling body changes, school and personal life balance increase the adolescents’ vulnerability to depression. 
[160] While some navigate this stage smoothly, some may not. Poor interpersonal skills, coupled with negative thought processes, can make it difficult for the young person to negotiate changes in relationships among the peers, families and the society. At this stage, they are searching for independence while also trying to fit in and at the same time, trying to succeed in their academics and maintain a level of balance in the social environment (Özdemir and Utkualp, 2016).  
[161] The causes of depression are quite complex during this stage with multiple contributing factors but some of the factors that can predispose a young person to depression are the biological risk factors (genetic composition), psychosocial risk factors and the environment (Zajkowska, Walsh, Zonca, Gullet, Pedersen, Kieling, Swartz, Karmacharya, Fisher, Kohrt and Mondelli, 2021). This is similar to the study by Wainberg, Jacobs, Voineskos and Tripathy (2022), which indicates that genetic composition is a very strong risk factor for depression. Children of parents who have depression such as maternal depression in the prenatal and postnatal period are three to four times likely to have depression when compared to others whose parents do not have depression. A history of parental depression is also associated with suicide attempts in adulthood (Thapar, Eyre, Patel and Brent, 2022). (Jaffee, Sligo, McAnally, Bolton, Baxter and Hancox  2020) in a longitudinal birth cohort study, compared parents who had depression and their offspring. The authors reported that parental depression significantly increases offspring risk for depression especially when it starts in adolescence. 
[162] There is also a strong link in the relationship between chronic medical or physical health conditions in adolescence and depression. According to Delamater, Guzman, and Aparicio (2017), adolescents with chronic illness have generally three to four times higher rates of mental health disorders than their healthy peers. Chronic medical conditions such as cancers, HIV/AIDS, diabetes, heart defects, obesity, asthma increases adolescents’ risks for depression. Some of these conditions require frequent and long term hospitalization which disconnects the adolescents from the social world and deprive them of the privilege of interacting with their peers. The side effects of medications from these illnesses may also be immense thus, fostering social withdrawal. Adolescents often have challenges adapting to the changes and demands of the chronic illness during this period.  Depression is the most common complication of almost all serious medical conditions. These illnesses come with great burden for adolescents and may make them susceptible to depression (Russo, K., 2022). In a study by Nouman, Memon, Nizamani, Ahmed, Kousar, and Khan, (2024), they found evidence to support the relationship between depression and asthma, being a chronic illness. A total of 262 asthma patients were included in the study, and it revealed that depression is common among  with asthma, and both depression and asthma often occur together. 
[163] Similarly, (Copeland, Alaie, Jonsson, and Shanahan, 2021) recognized the effect of early adolescent depression on future outcomes in a study involving 1,420 participants. They employed the structured Child and Adolescents Psychiatric Interview in assessing the participants. The authors noted that childhood or adolescent depression was associated with higher levels of adult anxiety, illicit drug use, criminal acts, impaired social functioning and the development of poorer health outcomes in various aspects of life and work impairment in adulthood. The authors concluded that early depression in childhood or adulthood have a great and lasting association with adults functioning. Early onset of depression in adolescents increases the risk for recurrent depressive episodes, preventing the adolescents from making choices that would benefit their lives in the long run especially if intervention is not done on time.
[164] Studies have shown an association between depression and the environment. Farella, and Gobbi, (2023), reported that factors such as acute stressful events; loss of a loved one, chronic poverty, child physical or sexual abuse, family dysfunction, academics stress, low self-esteem, relationship break-down, high expressed emotion, community violence, loss of a loved one, parental separation or divorce, bereavement play a significant role as risk factors for depression. The adolescent period is a period of vulnerability and the environment plays very important factor in the adolescent’s ability to navigate the period. Environmental factors such as abuse, family dysfunction, bullying severely impact the adolescent. These factors tend to interrupt several psychological pathways, they have long lasting negative emotional, physical and emotional impact of those affected. These factors make the victims experience intense sadness, persistent feeling of hopelessness, loss of interest in things they once found enjoyable, lower levels of perceived social support and increase negative thoughts and perception about self. Nevertheless, exposure to the psychosocial risk factors does not always lead to the development of depression but those with genetic history of depression may then be susceptible to the effects of these stressors.
[165] 2.5 Symptoms and Diagnosis of Depression.
[166] 2.5.1 Diagnostic criteria and Common symptoms for depression. 
[167] Depression in adolescent is a serious mood disorder that affects the whole body, moods and thoughts. In adolescents, it is common and it usually goes unidentified and untreated even among clinicians as some of them present with symptoms that may be behavioral or physical which may not be the typical symptoms of depression found in adults (Chavez, Sullivan, and Portela, 2023). Symptoms expression may vary from persons and with developmental stage. Children and adolescents may have difficulty in describing, identifying and expressing their internal mood (Favieri, Marini, and Casagrande, 2021). 
[168] Bell, Boyce, Porter, Bryant, and Malhi, (2021) reported in a study on irritability in mood disorders that feeling irritable is common and causes notable distress and impairment. In depression, irritability is ever present and plays a significant role in diagnosis. However, despite its commonness, it still remains poorly understood. Mullarkey, Marchetti, and Beevers (2020) followed up on 1,409 adolescents between the ages of 13 to 19 years. The authors reported that the most central symptoms of depression in this group were self-hatred, loneliness, sadness, and pessimism. The strongest association between these symptoms were sadness-crying, anhedonia, sadness, difficulty with school performance, self-hatred, sleep disturbance and self-blame. 
[169] To make a diagnosis of depression according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-Fifth Edition (DSM-5), the following must be put into consideration; the core symptoms, some associated symptoms (at least four should be present), pervasiveness (which means symptoms must be present every day for most of the day), duration (symptoms must have lasted for at least two weeks), impairment in functionality, symptoms are not due to substance or medical conditions. Depressive symptoms may be complex and expression of these symptoms may vary widely among persons, however, the core symptoms of depression are sadness, unhappiness, irritability and anhedonia (loss of interest in things that were formerly pleasurable) and associated symptoms include loss of energy, fatigue, feeling of worthlessness, significant weight loss when not dieting, inability to concentrate, persistent thoughts of suicide, psychomotor retardation (Rey, Bella-Awusah and Liu, 2015). Depression can also be expressed physically and some of these physical symptoms include moving or speaking more slowly than usual, constipation, lack of energy, changes in appetite which may lead to weight loss in decreased appetite and sometimes weight gain in increased appetite. Some social symptoms are withdrawal from social relationships; friends and families, isolation, and lack of motivation. In rare cases, people with severe depression may present with psychotic symptoms. The manifestation of depression may not be the same in all patients but they usually exhibit similar symptoms. Some adolescents may exhibit somatic symptoms which may not be the typical symptoms of depressive symptoms. These somatic symptoms may vary from patients. Some depressed patients complain of body pains, chest pains, which they do not describe as an ordinary pain but like a band around their head or chest, some also feel the pain around their abdomen or their entire body (Kapfhammer, 2022). 
[170] Liu, Tu, Zhang, Yang, Liu, Lei, Wu, Sun, and Zhang, (2021) found evidence that for patients who suffer from somatic symptoms of depression, other significant symptoms of depression may be hidden and the presence of multiple somatic symptoms is often indication to the complexity of depression. The authors investigated the severity of depression in adolescents with somatic symptoms and those without somatic symptoms. A total of 74 first-episode patients of depression and 70 healthy subjects were recruited in the study. Patients diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of somatic symptoms. The study revealed more severity in the somatic depression group than those without somatic symptoms. Adolescents often present with somatic symptoms such as tiredness and fatigue, pain in the abdomen and back, changes in vision, headaches, reduced pain tolerance, diarrhea. Somatic symptoms in adolescent depression can be misinterpreted for physical illness and when intervention is not channeled in the right direction, depression may worsen. It is important to educate educators on the core depressive symptoms and somatic symptoms so that thorough examination and proper diagnosis may be carried out. 
[171] 2.6 Mental Health Literacy and Its Importance
[172] Mental health disorders usually have their onset in adolescents or young adulthood. Early identification and intervention may improve the long–term outcome of these . When mental health help is sought late, it makes intervention difficult. When young people, their caregivers and the society at large have an understanding of mental health, it improves health seeking behavior and fosters overall outcome. Mental health literacy refers to an individual's knowledge, attitude and understanding of mental health disorders, including the ability to recognise symptoms, manage mental health problems, and seek     appropriate care. It also encompasses the reduction of stigma and the promotion of mental health awareness (Marinucci, Grové, and Allen, 2023).
[173] A recent mental health literacy training program for educators was conducted as part of a collaboration between the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), World Health Organization (WHO) and the Ministry of Health in Trinidad and Tobago. This three-day "Mental Health Literacy in Schools Trainer of Trainers" workshop took place from February 27 to March 1, 2024, in Port of Spain, involving 58 representatives from the Ministries of Education and Health, including regional health authorities (Pan American Health Organisation, 2024). The program aimed to enhance educators' capacity to promote mental health awareness, early identification, and intervention among students, with a focus on culturally relevant strategies. The findings from the training suggested the future importance of mental health literacy training to improve the mental health knowledge of educators as well as their attitude to mental health. 
[174] In Low and Middle Income countries like Nigeria, there is a great need for mental health literacy. The burden of mental illnesses in this region is high and these conditions are usually undiagnosed and untreated. In this region, there is limited access to mental health resources and care; there are few service centers available and due to cultural perception, these facilities may be stigmatised, hence, hindering people from seeking appropriate help. In the Low and middle income countries, the level of mental health awareness is low; many children and adolescents lack the basic knowledge of mental health conditions, their causes and management options (Carlson, Namy, Nakuti, Mufson, Ikenberg, Musoni, Hopson, Anton-Erxleben, Naker and Wainberg, 2021). In this region, the rate of stigma and discrimination around mental illnesses is quite high and families of individuals with mental illnesses do not seek appropriate help, this further worsens the condition. High mental health literacy among adolescents and those responsible for their care, such as educators and religious leaders, is crucial for early detection and intervention, stigma reduction and promotion of the adolescent’s wellbeing in cases of depression (Schulte-Körne, 2016). Mental health literacy is very crucial to reduce stigma; mental health education helps to eliminate myths and misconceptions around mental health, promote open conversations around mental illnesses and improve help seeking behaviours. It also provide support for individuals with mental illnesses, empowers individuals with knowledge of their own mental health, recognize symptoms early and access the mental health care. This further helps to promote early intervention which is the best form of intervention especially for adolescents. Mental health literacy fosters community support, improves the overall well-being of people with mental illnesses, and enhances economic growth and productivity. 
[175] Mental health literacy would improve the society’s understanding of mental health, help them accept mental health disorders, foster open conversations around the subject, support individuals with metal illnesses, create an environment that is supportive and thereby improving overall mental health outcomes of the society at large. Research suggests that individuals with higher levels of mental health literacy are more likely to seek help for mental health issues and less likely to hold stigmatising attitudes towards those with mental disorders (Marinucci, Grové, and Allen, 2023). For adolescents, increasing mental health literacy can foster  a supportive environment where they feel comfortable discussing their mental health challenges. Additionally, mental health literacy is essential for educators and mentors, as they are often the first point of contact for adolescents experiencing emotional distress (Aluh Anyachebelu, Anosike and Anizoba, 2018).
[177] 2.7 Training Programs for Mental Health Screening
[178] 2.7.1 Overview of training programs for educators.
[179] There has been evidence from trainings organized for educators and religious leaders on the impact that the acquisition of mental health knowledge has on their perception of mental illnesses as well as referral of individuals with these disorders to health care facilities. Wiedermann, Barbieri, Plagg, Marino, Piccoliori, and Engl, (2023) reported an increasing understanding in the role and impact of the educational sector toward the promotion of mental health and also as a pathway to care and support. Educators play vital roles in noting changes in their students, due to their day to day interactions. Beyond teaching students in classes, they also mentor, guide and inspire their students. They can play significant roles in early identification and intervention to mental illnesses in young people (Maclean and Law 2022). Mental health programs and trainings held in schools have been reported to help bridge treatment gaps between students with mental illnesses and treatment facilities especially in the rural communities (Moon, Williford and Mendenhall, 2017). 
[180] A Mental Health First Aid training program for secondary school educators was conducted by Fisher, Harding, Bell, Copeland, Evans, Powell, Araya, Campbell, Ford, Gunnell, Murphy and Kidger (2020). The intervention was conducted in 12 schools in England and it was aimed at improving the mental health of secondary school educators in order to reduce the risk of them developing more serious, longer-term mental health conditions. It was also done with the aim of negating the adverse impact of longer-term mental health conditions on educators-student relationships and learning outcomes of students that may accompany poor mental health in their educators. This was done basically to improve provision for children and adolescents’ mental health. 
[181] In their study of the Impact of Trauma-Based Training on Educators, Parker, Olson and Bunde (2020) also contributed to training educators on how to support and help adolescents who might have had a traumatic experience or the other at a point in their lives. The training included 219 participants, the study also revealed that majority of the attendees reported lasting changes in thinking and behavior as a result of the training. The training was done to improve the immediate outcome of the students by helping them academically thereby also improving their long term outcome. 
[183] Similar mental health training program was conducted by Atilola, Ayinde, Obialo, Adeyemo, Adegbaju, and Anthony, (2022), the training was conducted in schools across South-Western Nigeria. The authors reported that the health-education curriculum in Nigerian educational sector focused on hygiene hygiene and environmental health with hardly any significant mental-health content. The study revealed the low level of depression literacy among adolescents in Nigeria, which is the essential for symptom recognition, positive attitude and help-seeking behavior. It also revealed the limited knowledge about mental illness among educators which is a predictors to poor help seeking behavior among school-going adolescents. The aim of the study was to determine the impact of a school-based training program on depression-literacy among a cohort of high-school students and their educators in South-West Nigeria. The study recorded a total of 3098 students and 294 educators from 21 schools across three states in South-West Nigeria. The authors implemented an adapted version of the Break Free from Depression, a 4-module depression awareness curriculum for staff and students among the participants of the training. The study revealed a significant positive difference at post-test in the knowledge, attitude, and confidence among the students and the same among the educators. The authors concluded that School-based depression-literacy programs can lead to significant positive change in knowledge, attitude, and confidence of students and educators. 
[185] In religious settings there has been studies aimed at improving religious leaders’ knowledge of mental illness in order to improve the outcome of members who may present with these conditions. In a study among religious leaders in Peruvian city, Cjuno, Zafra-Tanaka, Pilar Garcı´a Garcı´a and Taype-Rondan (2023) sought to understand the perceptions of religious leaders in identifying and referring members with mental health issues to health care services. They completed a survey that assessed their characteristics, past experiences of detecting and referring members with mental disorders to health care professionals. The study which was conducted among 104 leaders of four religious groups revealed that half of the leaders had reported that at least one of their members had approached them for mental health help in the previous month. The study also revealed that more than 85% of the religious leaders identified depression and problematic alcohol and drug use correctly when they were asked to identify the cases. The study also indicated that most of the leaders were willing to refer their members to services for proper help and care. This study suggests that when leaders are well equipped with understanding of mental disorders they would be willing to support and refer members who may present with these cases.
[186] Educators in every level are often in a position of influnce in their students’ lives and their own understanding of subjects like mental illnesses may have effect of the students who may present with these diorders either positively or negatively. When religious educators are well equipped to recognise certain disorders, it increases the chance for early intervention. This further helps them to provide the right support and make referral if necessary, thereby, improving the long term outcome of these students. 
[187] Although school settings have been identified as ideal settings to reach children in need of mental health services, there is still a need to create mental health awareness in every sector (Pryde, 2021), with the aim of improving the quality of life of adolescents who in turn become adults.
[189] 2.8 The Role of Religious Institutions in Mental Health Support
[190] Historically, religion has played a central role of human lives, shaping personal identity and creating a string connection to a wider community (Pastwa-Wojciechowska, Grzegorzewska, and Wojciechowska, 2021). Religious activities such as fasting, praying, meditation, services, trust in God affects the meaning of life positively. Religion has been observed to shape the psychosocial and moral development of young people and also linked to positive mental health outcomes among adults (Aggarwal, Wright, Morgan, Patton, and Reavley, 2023). Before the development of modern mental health systems, care for individuals with emotional or psychological difficulties was largely provided by religious groups (Kao, Peteet, and Cook, 2020). Religious institutions foster social support, healthy behaviours, coping strategies, and cognitive framework (Siqueira, Fernandes, and Moreira-Almeida, 2018). Engagement in religious practices is often associated with protection from several adverse health conditions. 
[191] In a study by Herdiansyah, Farghobi, Azmi, Khoiriyah, and Apriliani, (2024), they found that involvement in religious institutions, including practices, teachings, communal activities, influence mental health outcomes substantially and enhance psychological well-being. In another study by Claudes, Dewi, Heng, and Canesares, (2025), it was reported that the support provided by religious institutions contributes to better anxiety management among adolescents by building resilience, improving their mindset, encouraging effective coping practices and thereby promoting healthier coping behaviors.  In countries with limited mental health infrastructure, like Nigeria, churches and other religious organisations are often trusted sources of guidance for families, including those dealing  with mental health issues (Aluh, Anyachebelu, Anosike and Anizoba, 2018).
[192] Religious institutions, particularly churches, play a significant role in the lives of many adolescents in Nigeria. They provide not only spiritual guidance but also social support, and they can serve as a key platform for addressing mental health challenges within communities (Perez, Cardenas, Blagg, and Wong). Despite the influence of religious institutions, there remains a gap in utilising this potential to support adolescents with mental health problems, particularly depression. Studies suggest that religious leaders and educators in church settings are often not equipped with the necessary skills to recognise or address mental health concerns (Adewunmi, 2023). It was reported in a study by Duke, (2016), that in Nigeria, religious institutions function as an important agent, shaping individuals’ beliefs and behaviours in ways that influence mental health. It also underscores the importance of partnership between healthcare practitioners and church leaders. Training these individuals to identify symptoms of mental disorders like depression can help bridge the gap in mental health services, particularly in rural and under-served areas where professional mental health care may not be readily accessible.
[193] Furthermore, religious institutions can play a critical role in reducing the stigma associated with mental health disorders. By promoting open discussions about mental health within their communities, churches can help create an environment where adolescents feel comfortable seeking help without fear of judgment (Alukagberie, Elmusharaf, Ibrahim and Poix 2023).
[195] 2.9 Effectiveness of Mental Health Interventions in Religious Settings
[196] Several studies have shown that mental health interventions delivered within religious settings can be effective in addressing mental health disorders, especially when tailored to the cultural and social context of the community (Codjoe, Barber, Ahuja, Thornicroft, Henderson, Lempp, and N’Danga-Koroma, 2021). Interventions that focus on training religious leaders and community members to recognise and respond to mental health issues have proven successful in increasing mental health literacy and reducing the stigma associated with mental illness (Adewunmi, 2023).
[197] One study conducted in Kenya found that training clergy members in mental health awareness significantly improved their ability to identify individuals struggling with depression (Christy, Ndetei, Mutiso, Musyimi, Abednego, Nandoya, Anderson, Milanovic, Henderson, and McKenzie 2017). The programme not only enhanced the clergy’s confidence in addressing mental health issues but also led to increased referrals for professional care, bridging the gap in mental health services in under-served areas.
[198] Similarly, in the United States, church-based mental health programmes have been shown to improve community members’ knowledge of mental health, reduce stigmatising attitudes, and increase help-seeking behaviour (Riddick 2024). These findings suggest that religious institutions can serve as valuable platforms for mental health interventions, particularly in communities where access to formal mental health services is limited.
[199] However, challenges remain in implementing such programmes. Religious leaders often lack formal training in mental health, and there can be resistance to addressing mental health issues due to cultural or religious beliefs that view mental illness as a spiritual, rather than medical, issue (Aluh, Anyachebelu, Anosike and Anizoba, 2018). Overcoming these barriers requires culturally sensitive approaches that integrate mental health education with religious teachings.
[201] 2.10 Challenges in Implementing Mental Health Interventions in Religious Settings
[202] While religious institutions have the potential to play a crucial role in mental health support, there are several challenges that hinder the effective implementation of mental health interventions within these settings. One of the primary obstacles is the lack of formal mental health training for religious leaders and mentors. Without adequate training, these individuals may struggle to recognise mental health disorders such as depression, and may not be equipped to refer individuals to appropriate professional care (Aluh, Anyachebelu, Anosike and Anizoba, 2018).
[203] Cultural and religious beliefs also present significant barriers. In many communities, mental health issues are often viewed through a spiritual lens, with some religious leaders attributing disorders like depression to spiritual weakness or demonic possession (Christy, Ndetei, Mutiso, Musyimi, Abednego, Nandoya, Anderson, Milanovic, Henderson, and McKenzie 2017). Such beliefs can prevent adolescents and their families from seeking professional mental health care, as they may be encouraged to rely solely on prayer or spiritual healing. Addressing these challenges requires culturally sensitive approaches that respect religious beliefs while promoting mental health literacy and the importance of professional care.
[205] Additionally, stigma remains a persistent issue in religious communities. Adolescents with mental health disorders often face judgement and discrimination, which can discourage them from seeking help (Alukagberie, Elmusharaf, Ibrahim and Poix, 2023). Efforts to reduce stigma must be central to any mental health intervention in religious settings, with a focus on education and awareness-raising initiatives that challenge negative perceptions of mental illness.
[209] 2.11 Summary of Literature Review 
[210] The reviewed literature highlights the critical importance of addressing mental health issues among adolescents, particularly depression. These disorders are prevalent worldwide, including in Nigeria, where socio-economic challenges and cultural barriers exacerbate the problem. Adolescents suffering from depression face significant academic, social, and emotional difficulties, and without early intervention, these conditions can lead to severe long-term consequences, including chronic mental health problems and suicidal behavior.
[212] The literature also underscores the role of mental health literacy in promoting early detection and treatment. Increased mental health awareness among educators of adolescents and religious leaders can help bridge the gap in mental health services, particularly in under-served areas where access to professional care is limited. Religious institutions, due to their influence in many communities, present an untapped resource for delivering mental health interventions. Studies have shown that training church leaders and mentors in mental health can improve their ability to recognise and refer adolescents with mental health issues, contributing to better outcomes.
[214] However, the review also highlights several challenges in implementing mental health interventions in religious settings. Cultural and religious beliefs often hinder the recognition and treatment of mental health disorders, and stigma remains a major barrier. Addressing these challenges requires culturally sensitive approaches that integrate mental health education with religious teachings, reduce stigma, and encourage help-seeking behaviour among adolescents.
[216] In conclusion, the literature review provides a strong foundation for this study, which aims to assess the effectiveness of a mental health training programme for religious educators in Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria. By improving mental health literacy and reducing stigma, the study seeks to enhance the capacity of religious institutions to support adolescents with depression.

[275] 3. Sampling Technique
[277] A preliminary mapping was conducted. This involved seeking recommendations from personal contacts, community leaders, church networks, and conducting brief informal interviews with church leaders and youth coordinators to confirm the presence of structured adolescent-focused activities. Based on this process, churches that had ongoing programmes targeting adolescents such as youth churches, teenage Sunday school classes or mentorship initiatives were shortlisted. Religious educators from these churches were then invited to participate in the study. This approach was appropriate as the study required participants who were directly involved in mentoring adolescents and had the potential to benefit from the mental health training programme. The sample size consisted of 50 adolescent educators, selected from four churches across different    regions of Ibadan South West Local Government Area (LGA), Oyo State to ensure diversity in the sample. The sample size was determined based on logistical considerations and the need for sufficient representation from different church settings.
[307] 3. Ethical Considerations 
[308] Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Oyo-State Ministry of Health ethical review committee, with Ref No. AD 13/479/617 and permission acquired from each church leaders. This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines set by the Oyo-State Ministry of Health ethical review committee. The following ethical considerations were addressed throughout the study.
[309] 3..1 Informed Consent
[310] Before participating in the study, all adolescent educators were provided with detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Participants were informed that their involvement was voluntary and that they may withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the commencement of the study.
[312] 3..2 Confidentiality
[313] The confidentiality of participants was strictly maintained. All data collected were anonymised to protect participants’ identities. Personal information, including names and contact   details, were stored securely. The interview transcripts and assessment were coded using unique identifiers to ensure that individual responses could not be traced back to specific participants.
[315] 3..3 Minimising Harm
[316] The mental health training programme was designed to avoid causing psychological distress to participants. Although the programme involved discussions on depression which was a sensitive topic, it was conducted in a supportive and non-judgmental environment. Plans were put in place for participant who may experience distress during the programme, to be offered appropriate support and referral to mental health professionals if necessary. 
[319] 3..4 Debriefing
[320] At the conclusion of the study, a debriefing session was held with participants to explain the study’s findings and discuss any remaining questions or concerns. Participants were also informed about available mental health resources and services in their communities, in case of further support.
[325] 3.8 Data Analysis and Management
[327] The quantitative data collected from the pre-test and post-test assessments was analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Descriptive statistics, such as means, percentages, and standard deviations, were used to summarise the participants’ baseline knowledge and post-training improvements. Inferential statistics, including paired t-tests, was employed to determine whether the changes in participants’ mental health literacy and screening abilities were statistically significant. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, indicating that the training programme had a measurable effect on the participants' ability to screen for depression.Data management and analysis involved cleaning, coding, and entering responses, followed by descriptive and inferential statistical analysis. Data management procedures involved checking for errors, inconsistencies and missing responses. Cases with incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the analysis to ensure accuracy and reliability of results. Descriptive statistics, such as means and standard deviations, were computed, while inferential statistics including paired t-tests, were conducted to examine differences within the intervention group by comparing pre- and post- test scores. 
[335] 3.9 Validity and Reliability
[336] Content validity was established by reviewing relevant literature to refine the questionnaire. Criterion validity was tested by correlating pre- and post-training scores with external indicators like confidence levels and referral willingness, confirming that the training effectively improved participants’ knowledge and skills.
[337] Test-retest reliability was assessed through Pearson’s correlation, confirming stability over time. Paired t-tests, ANOVA, and Chi-square tests validated score changes across education levels and prior training experiences, reinforcing the reliability of the assessment tools.
These measures ensured that the study’s instruments were both accurate and consistent, strengthening the credibility of the findings.
